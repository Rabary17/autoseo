***Braking that triggers on its own is first a safety matter. It is also a practical one: what does the system do, when does it act, and what is still your job?*** The European regulation known as GSR2 (General Safety Regulation 2) now sets a precise framework for new cars, rolled out over several years.

TL;DR: automatic emergency braking has detected other vehicles since 2024. From 7 July 2026, it must also detect pedestrians and cyclists on every new car sold in the European Union.

-   **New models already had to detect** pedestrians and cyclists from July 2024.
-   From **7 July 2026**, this requirement extends to every new car registered, including models already on sale.
-   Cars already on the road have **no obligation to be upgraded**.

## What already applies since 2024

Since July 2024, new type-approved cars must include automatic emergency braking able to detect other vehicles, plus a first level of pedestrian and cyclist detection for newly type-approved models. The principle stays simple: the system spots an obstacle ahead, warns the driver, then brakes automatically if a collision becomes imminent and nothing has been done to avoid it.

This regulatory base, part of EU regulation 2019/2144, first helped reduce rear-end collisions, among the most frequent in town. Small series, generally under 1,500 vehicles a year, follow their own timetable but remain subject to the same core safety obligations, sometimes with staggered deadlines.

## What changes on 7 July 2026

From 7 July 2026, pedestrian and cyclist detection becomes mandatory for every new car registered, not just newly type-approved models. In practice, a model that has been on sale for several years, but is still produced and sold new, must now also include this wider detection.

The regulation also requires stronger pedestrian protection in a crash, including against head injuries, for passenger cars and light commercial vehicles. Vans also gain better forward visibility for the driver, a point long criticised by road-safety groups because of blind spots on light commercials.

Another feature arrives alongside this deadline: the Emergency Stop Signal, or **ESS**. It makes the brake lights flash rapidly during hard braking or when ABS kicks in, warning the car behind more quickly.

| Milestone | What's required | Who's affected |
| --- | --- | --- |
| From 2024 | Detection of other vehicles; first-level pedestrian and cyclist detection | Newly type-approved models |
| From 7 July 2026 | Full pedestrian and cyclist detection, ESS, driver-attention monitoring | Every new car registered, including existing models still on sale |

This staggered timetable explains why two cars of the same age can carry different equipment: what matters is the registration date, not the model's launch date.

## Driver attention monitoring

The most visible new feature on board is a camera placed behind the steering wheel, in the steering column or on the dashboard. It watches where the driver is looking and detects signs of prolonged inattention. Between 20 and 50 km/h, an audible alert sounds after several seconds of continuous inattention. Above 50 km/h, the thresholds tighten and alerts become more frequent.

The system activates automatically every time the vehicle starts. The driver can switch it off manually, so it partly depends on your own settings. A disabled system is still a choice you made, and it never excuses inattention behind the wheel.

This monitoring often comes with an intelligent speed limiter that reads road signs, plus a lane-departure warning. Together, these form the technical baseline the regulation requires for every new car sold in the EU from 2026, on top of the [SAE autonomy levels](/sae-driving-levels-what-your-car-can-do/) already found on some recent models.

## Is your current car affected?

No. The regulation targets new vehicles only. Owners of cars already registered have no obligation to upgrade, even if their model lacks these features.

If you change vehicle after 7 July 2026, the new model you buy must include all these systems, whether it is a brand-new launch or a generation that has been on sale for years. None of this applies retroactively to a used car registered before that date, and the MOT test does not penalise its absence.

## What this means for insurance and maintenance

These systems add sensors, cameras and extra control units to the vehicle. After even a minor knock, a windscreen or bumper fitted with sensors often costs more to repair than a standard part, which can affect certain cover premiums. Ask your insurer about replacement costs for this equipment before signing a policy for a recent new car.

On the maintenance side, a windscreen that is badly recalibrated after replacement can throw off the automatic braking system's detection. Always have the cameras and radars recalibrated after any work on the windscreen, the front bumper or the steering column, by a professional equipped for that job. For an imported car, check this point alongside the [documents needed to register it](/register-imported-vehicle-france/), since these sensors are not yet part of a standard inspection.

## Buying used: how to check these features

On a recent used vehicle, the presence of automatic emergency braking is usually listed on the original spec sheet or in the manufacturer's handbook. Ask for a test drive to check that the collision alert triggers normally, and that the attention-monitoring camera isn't lit permanently, a sign of malfunction. If the car was imported, confirm its [European Certificate of Conformity](/european-coc-vehicle-registration/) matches, since driver-assistance features are not detailed on a standard MOT.

## Frequently asked questions

### Does my current car need to be upgraded?

No. Vehicles already registered are not covered by this deadline. The regulation applies to new cars sold and registered from the dates set by the European text, not to vehicles already on the road.

### Does automatic braking replace the driver?

No. The system warns first, then brakes only if a collision becomes imminent. It cannot anticipate every driving situation, and responsibility for driving stays entirely with you.

### Are small series affected by this rule?

Yes, but on their own timetable. Their vehicles must still meet the mandatory active-safety systems, including automatic braking, lane keeping and sign recognition.

### What exactly does the Emergency Stop Signal (ESS) do?

It makes the brake lights flash rapidly during hard braking or an ABS intervention. This warns the following driver faster and reduces the risk of a rear-end collision.

### Can you switch off driver-attention monitoring?

Yes, manually, on each trip. The system reactivates by default the next time you start the car. Switching it off is a driver's choice, not a permanent removal of the equipment.

### Does automatic braking work at night or in the rain?

Sensors and cameras lose reliability in poor visibility, heavy rain or thick fog. The system remains a complementary safety net, never an absolute guarantee whatever the driving conditions.

### Does the rule apply the same way to vans and light commercial vehicles?

Mostly, yes. Light commercial vehicles follow the same detection and pedestrian-protection requirements as passenger cars, with the added obligation of better forward visibility from the driver's seat to cut down blind spots.

### Will my insurance premium rise because of this equipment?

Not automatically, but the parts themselves cost more to repair. A windscreen or bumper fitted with cameras and sensors is pricier than a standard one, which some insurers reflect in certain cover options rather than in the base premium.
