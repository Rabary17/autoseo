***At the dealership, "automatic gearbox" gets treated as a single block. In the workshop, we see three distinct mechanisms that do not age the same way.*** Knowing which one sits under your bonnet avoids surprises at the first service.

TL;DR: the classic automatic is the smoothest and most forgiving of imperfect servicing. The dual-clutch reacts fast but needs a regular oil change. The CVT is quiet and economical, with a driving feel that surprises many people at first.

-   **Classic automatic**: smooth shifts, simple servicing that any garage knows well.
-   **Dual-clutch (DSG)**: very fast shifts, genuinely sensitive to maintenance.
-   **CVT**: continuous variation, no fixed ratios, a particular feel under acceleration.

## Three mechanisms, three ways of working

The **classic automatic** relies on a torque converter and a set of planetary gears. It shifts smoothly and copes well with hill starts or towing. It is the oldest and most common design, fitted across generations of cars for decades.

The **dual-clutch**, known as DSG in its Volkswagen Group form, uses two clutches operated alternately: while one gear is engaged, the next is already prepared. This makes shifts very fast, almost imperceptible on the road. In exchange, some versions can judder at low speed, especially in traffic or while parking.

The **CVT**, or continuously variable transmission, has no fixed ratios. It adjusts the ratio constantly through pulleys and a metal belt. The engine often stays in its most fuel-efficient range, but the driving feel differs noticeably from a classic gearbox: acceleration doesn't come with the usual kick of a gear change.

None of the three is inherently "better": each was designed for a different priority. The classic automatic favours comfort and durability over outright efficiency. The dual-clutch trades a touch of low-speed smoothness for sharper responses and lower fuel use than a traditional torque converter. The CVT chases fuel economy above all, accepting a driving feel that takes some getting used to in exchange.

## What changes day to day

In town, the classic automatic fades into the background: shifts are gradual and predictable. The dual-clutch responds more sharply to the accelerator, which suits drivers who like responsiveness, but can feel less smooth at a standstill or crawling in traffic. The CVT is discreet and easy-going, though it can feel like slipping under hard acceleration, as engine revs climb before road speed catches up.

On the open road, the gap widens. The dual-clutch holds up well on overtaking and pulling away. The CVT favours fuel economy, at the cost of a less direct driving feel. ## Maintenance and reliability, the point that matters

The dual-clutch needs gearbox oil changes done properly, with the fluid specified by the manufacturer, typically every 60,000 km. A neglected unit can wear out early, as the clutch discs don't tolerate the excess heat from degraded fluid. The CVT also needs a specific fluid: mixing a generic product with the original fluid damages the belt and pulleys.

The classic automatic is the most tolerant of less rigorous servicing, though it stays sensitive to oil that has aged past its useful life. In every case, ask for the full oil-change history before buying a used car; that paperwork matters more than the mileage on the clock.

A full gearbox replacement is costly whichever technology you pick, but the gap between them is real. A dual-clutch rebuild or replacement often runs higher than a classic automatic's, mostly because of the two clutch packs and their dedicated control unit. A CVT repair depends heavily on belt and pulley availability for the specific model, which can push out both the cost and the wait for parts. None of this should discourage a purchase; it simply makes the service book the single most important document in the deal.

## Which choice for your use and budget

If you drive mostly in town and want a car without surprises, the **classic automatic** suits you best: it forgives imperfect servicing more readily. If you enjoy sharp acceleration and accept strict, well-documented maintenance, the **dual-clutch** may please you, provided you check the service book on a used example. If fuel economy on regular trips is your priority, the **CVT** is a good candidate, as long as you like its particular feel and respect the specified fluid.

Before signing for a used car, an extended test drive remains the best check: accelerate firmly, drive in dense traffic, then on a fast road. A gearbox that slips, clunks or responds sluggishly should raise concern, even though the MOT test doesn't specifically cover the transmission.

### Is the DSG a fragile gearbox?

Not in itself. A dual-clutch is reliable when serviced properly. Problems mostly come from a missed oil change or very demanding driving at low speed, such as prolonged traffic jams.

### Is a CVT less reliable than a classic automatic?

Not necessarily, but it needs a specific fluid and consistent servicing. Check the car's history before deciding; mixing fluids is the most common cause of early failure.

## Frequently asked questions

### Do you need an automatic gearbox for city driving?

Many drivers find an automatic more comfortable in traffic. The choice depends mainly on your maintenance budget and how much you enjoy driving.

### Can you tell the gearbox type from the vehicle's spec sheet?

Yes, the registration certificate and manufacturer spec sheet usually state the transmission type: torque converter, dual-clutch or continuously variable.

### Does a CVT cost more to replace than a classic gearbox?

Generally yes, since a CVT uses specific parts (belt, pulleys) that are less widely available than a classic planetary gear set.

### Does a dual-clutch use more fuel than a classic gearbox?

No, it's usually the opposite: the DSG optimises gear changes to limit consumption while keeping strong responsiveness.

### Which gearbox should you choose for towing a caravan or trailer?

The classic automatic remains the reference for towing, thanks to a torque converter that copes better with sustained low-speed loads.

### How do you recognise the gearbox type before a test drive?

The spec sheet or an online search by registration number usually states it. On the road, a dual-clutch shifts almost instantly with a faint double click, a CVT holds a steady engine note under acceleration, and a classic automatic shifts with a gentle, perceptible nudge.

### Is it worth paying more for a low-mileage used car with a dual-clutch gearbox?

Often yes, provided the service book proves the oil changes were done on schedule. A dual-clutch with a gap in its maintenance history can cost more to fix than the price difference saved at purchase.
