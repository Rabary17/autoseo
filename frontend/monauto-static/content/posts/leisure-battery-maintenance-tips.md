***A leisure battery powers the fridge, lights and heating in a motorhome off-grid. Poorly maintained, it can die in a single season. Properly cared for, it lasts five to ten years depending on the technology.***

A weekend in a campervan in the Cévennes, or three weeks of road-tripping across Scotland, puts far more strain on a leisure battery than on the engine battery. It takes deep charge cycles, winter cold and sometimes months of standing idle. Here's how to maintain it, choose the right technology and prepare it for winter storage.

TL;DR: a leisure battery lasts longer when it avoids deep discharges, stays clean at the terminals and goes into winter storage charged to 100%.

-   A lead-acid battery should never drop below **50% charge**.
-   Replacement costs **€100 to €900** depending on the technology chosen.
-   A lithium LiFePO4 battery handles two to three times more cycles than a classic lead-acid one.

## Why does a leisure battery wear out faster than an engine battery?

The engine battery benefits from regular journeys to recharge via the alternator. The leisure battery depends instead on solar panels, a 230V mains hook-up or a split-charge relay, sources that are far less constant.

It also takes deeper charge and discharge cycles. Ageing speeds up as soon as charge regularly drops below 50%. Damp and cold make it worse: a battery left in an uninsulated compartment over winter can freeze, especially if already weakened. The internal plates then warp and capacity drops sharply, sometimes for good.

## Which battery technology should you choose?

Three families equip most motorhomes and campervans, each with its own demands and budget.

| Technology | Typical price | Strengths | Limits |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Flooded lead-acid | €100–200 | Lowest purchase price | Needs regular upkeep, dislikes deep discharges |
| AGM | €200–400 | Maintenance-free, tolerates tilted positions | Pricier, fewer cycles than lithium |
| Lithium (LiFePO4) | €400–900 | Deep discharges without damage, fast charging, light | High upfront cost, check cold-weather protection |

For occasional trips with frequent mains hook-ups, an **AGM** is more than enough. For long off-grid stays or winter travel, **lithium** handles repeated cycles and cold better, provided you pick a model with protection against charging below 0°C, a key point for this technology.

## Routine maintenance: simple steps that make all the difference

**Three habits are enough: avoid discharges below 50%, recharge with a charger suited to the technology, and clean the terminals regularly against corrosion.**

A lead-acid battery, standard or AGM, should never drop below 50% charge. Below that, sulphate builds up on the plates and permanently reduces capacity. For a long trip with no mains access, a solar panel or a backup generator keeps the level up.

Recharging also deserves attention. An AGM or gel battery doesn't like fast charging; a voltage of 14.4V to 14.8V for a 12V model remains the reference. Check that the motorhome's built-in charger is set correctly for your battery type before a long spell on mains power.

**Corrosion at the terminals** creates resistance that prevents a proper recharge. A quick pass with a wire brush followed by a little neutral grease fixes this in a few minutes. For an open (flooded) battery, checking the electrolyte level every three months, topped up with distilled water without exceeding the maximum mark, prevents the plates from drying out.

## Monitoring your battery day to day

A battery monitor, or even a simple voltmeter on the dashboard, can save you from unpleasant surprises. Check it before switching off the lights at night and again on waking: a big gap between the two readings points to unusual draw, often a water pump or fridge fan left running by mistake.

The **split-charge relay**, which links the engine and leisure batteries, also deserves regular checking. If misadjusted, it can let the leisure battery drain the engine battery at rest, risking a dead start. A visual check of the connections and a multimeter test at each service rules this out before it strikes miles from any garage. Checking the vehicle's [waterproofing](/motorhome-waterproofing-check-guide/) at the same time also protects the battery compartment from leaks.

## Winter storage: steps to avoid a dead battery in spring

Winter storage is when most leisure batteries give up the ghost. Three steps limit this risk.

-   **Drain all tanks**: fresh water, grey water, water heater. A single drop of frozen water can crack a pipe, and ambient damp speeds up terminal corrosion.
-   **Cut the power**: disconnect the leisure battery and store it somewhere dry, away from frost. If it stays in place, an insulating blanket limits temperature swings.
-   **Charge to 100% before storing**: a battery left half-charged sulphates faster. A smart charger maintains an optimal level through the cold season.

Even disconnected, a lead-acid battery keeps slowly self-discharging over winter. A mid-season check, especially for a vehicle left unused for more than three months, lets you top it up before it drops too low. For the whole vehicle, our [winterisation checklist](/motorhome-winterisation-checklist/) covers every step, battery included.

## When should you replace it?

Even with careful maintenance, a leisure battery eventually loses capacity. Three signs don't lie: the fridge or lights cut out sooner than before despite a full charge, the voltage measured with a multimeter drops below 12V after a night with no draw, or the battery takes longer to recharge and heats up unusually.

Before replacing a battery that seems to be failing, first check it isn't a loose connection or a badly set charger. These two causes, far more common than a genuinely dead battery, are fixed in a few minutes and avoid an unnecessary expense. A voltage test when cold, then after a full charge, quickly tells you whether it's a charging issue or a battery at the end of its life. Worth checking before any purchase, whether you're buying [new or used](/used-motorhome-buying-guide/), and worth factoring into a used vehicle's expected [first-year depreciation](/motorhome-depreciation-first-year/) if the battery clearly needs replacing soon after purchase.

A well-maintained leisure battery also makes gas appliances safer to run off-grid, since a weak battery can affect the electronics that monitor your [gas system](/motorhome-gas-safety-guide/). And whatever the technology, respect the loaded weight limits covered in our [motorhome weight and licence guide](/motorhome-weight-licence-guide/): a second or heavier battery bank adds payload you need to account for.

## Frequently asked questions

### How far can you discharge a lead-acid leisure battery?

Not below 50% charge. Below that, sulphate builds up on the plates and permanently reduces the battery's capacity.

### How much does replacing a leisure battery cost?

Between €100 and €200 for a lead-acid battery, €200 to €400 for an AGM, and €400 to €900 for a lithium LiFePO4, installation included in 15 to 30 minutes.

### Should you disconnect the leisure battery for winter?

Yes, ideally. Disconnected, charged to 100% and stored somewhere dry away from frost, it copes far better with winter than a battery left in an uninsulated compartment.

### Does a lithium battery handle the cold?

Yes, but only with a model fitted with protection against charging below 0°C. Without this protection, charging a lithium battery in freezing conditions damages it permanently.

### How do you tell whether a fault comes from the battery or the charger?

Measure the voltage when cold, then after a full charge. A normal gap after charging points to a charger or connection issue; voltage that stays low after charging points to a battery at the end of its life.

### How often should you check the electrolyte level?

Every three months for an open lead-acid battery, topped up with distilled water without exceeding the maximum mark. AGM and lithium batteries need no such check.
