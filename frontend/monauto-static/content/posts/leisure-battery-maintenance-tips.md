A weekend in a van in the Cévennes, or a three-week road trip in a motorhome across Scotland: your leisure battery keeps your fridge cold, your lights on and your heating running when you’re off-grid. Yet it wears out faster than you might think. Here’s how to maintain it, choose the right technology and prepare it for winter storage—without the hassle.

## Why does a leisure battery wear out faster than an engine battery?

A leisure battery isn’t subject to the same demands as an engine battery. The latter benefits from regular journeys to recharge via the alternator, while the leisure battery relies on solar panels, a 230V mains hook-up or a split-charge relay.

It undergoes deeper charge/discharge cycles, which speeds up ageing. Another weak point: damp and cold. A battery left in an uninsulated compartment over winter can freeze—especially if it’s already weak. As a result, the internal plates warp and capacity plummets.

## Which battery technology should you choose?

Three types of batteries are commonly used in motorhomes and campervans, each with its own maintenance requirements.

-   **Flooded lead-acid (wet cell)**: the cheapest to buy, but also the most demanding. It requires regular electrolyte level checks and doesn’t handle repeated deep discharges well.
-   **AGM**: maintenance-free, it copes better with frequent cycles and tilted positions. More expensive than flooded lead-acid, but more reliable over time.
-   **Lithium (LiFePO4)**: lighter, it tolerates much deeper discharges without damage and recharges faster. Though the most expensive upfront, its lifespan in charge cycles far exceeds that of lead-acid.

The choice depends on how you use your vehicle. For occasional trips with frequent mains hook-ups, an AGM is more than adequate. For long off-grid stays or winter travel, lithium handles repeated cycles and cold better—provided you pick a model with protection against charging below 0°C, a key consideration for this technology.

## Routine maintenance: simple steps that make all the difference

You don’t need to be an expert to extend your leisure battery’s life. A few simple habits go a long way.

-   **Avoid deep discharges**: a lead-acid battery (standard or AGM) should never drop below 50% charge. Below that, sulphate builds up on the plates, reducing capacity. If you’re heading off-grid for an extended trip, pack a solar panel or generator to keep levels up.
-   **Recharge it properly**: AGM or gel batteries don’t like fast charging. Use a suitable charger with a voltage of 14.4V to 14.8V for a 12V battery. If you’re plugging into a 230V supply, check that your onboard charger is set for your battery type.

**Clean the terminals**: corrosion on the terminals creates resistance, preventing the battery from charging properly. A quick brush with a wire brush and a bit of neutral grease will sort it.

**Check the electrolyte level (for flooded batteries)**: if your battery isn’t sealed, check the electrolyte level every three months. Top up with distilled water if needed, but never exceed the max mark.

If you often use your motorhome in winter, opt for an AGM or gel battery. They handle the cold better than standard lead-acid batteries and require no maintenance. ## Monitoring your battery daily: good habits

A battery monitor (or even a simple voltmeter) on your dashboard can save you from nasty surprises. Check it before turning off the lights at night and again in the morning: a big difference between the two readings suggests something’s left on—like a water pump or fridge fan.

Another thing to watch: the split-charge relay connecting the engine and leisure batteries. If it’s faulty or misadjusted, it can let the leisure battery drain the engine battery while the vehicle’s parked—leaving you stranded when you try to start up. A quick visual check of the connections and a multimeter test during each service will help you spot issues before they ruin your trip.

## Winter storage: steps to avoid a dead battery in spring

![Leisure battery wrapped in an insulating blanket, stored on a wooden shelf in a garage](/images/articles/batterie-cellule-entretien-1.webp)

Winter storage is when many leisure batteries give up the ghost. Here’s how to avoid a nasty surprise come spring.

-   **Drain all tanks**: fresh water, grey water, water heater. A single drop of frozen water can crack a pipe. Ambient damp speeds up terminal corrosion.
-   **Cut the power**: disconnect the leisure battery and store it somewhere dry, away from frost. If you can’t move it, wrap it in an insulating blanket to limit temperature swings.
-   **Charge it to 100% before storing**: a battery left half-charged will sulphate faster. Use a smart charger to maintain optimal levels over winter.

One often-overlooked detail: even when disconnected, a lead-acid battery slowly self-discharges over winter. Plan a mid-season check, especially if your motorhome sits unused for more than three months—top it up if needed before it drops too low.

## When should you replace it?

Even with perfect maintenance, a leisure battery will eventually lose capacity. Here are the tell-tale signs.

-   Your fridge or lights cut out sooner than before, even after a full charge.
-   The voltage drops below 12V overnight with no power draw.
-   The battery takes longer to recharge or gets unusually hot during charging.

A replacement costs between €100 and €300, depending on the model. Installation takes 15 to 30 minutes. If you’re torn between types, [our comparison of AGM, gel and lithium batteries](/en/motorhomes-campervans/motorhome-waterproofing-check-guide) will help you decide.

Before replacing a battery that seems weak, first check for a loose connection or a misconfigured charger—both far more common than a truly dead battery. A quick voltage test when cold, then after a full charge, will tell you whether it’s a charging issue or a battery on its way out.
