***Two chemistries dominate electric car batteries, LFP and NMC. They age differently and don't call for the same charging habits.***

Open the file of an electric car and the spec sheet talks about range and power. It rarely says which chemistry sits under the floor. Yet that chemistry decides how the battery ages and what you should do to protect it.

TL;DR: LFP costs less and copes better with full charges. NMC stores more energy for the same weight, but needs more thermal care.

-   LFP: more **robust**, less energy-dense, often cheaper to buy.
-   NMC: more **range** for the same weight, more sensitive to heat.
-   In both cases, the pack's **thermal management** matters as much as the chemistry.

## Two chemistries, two philosophies

LFP uses iron and phosphate. It handles repeated charging cycles well and stays **stable** when overheated, with no sharp thermal runaway risk. Its main drawback is lower energy density: for the same weight, it stores less energy than an NMC. That's why LFP packs tend to show up on shorter-range, lower-priced models, where robustness matters more than squeezing out the last few kilometres.

NMC combines nickel, manganese and cobalt. It packs more **energy** into the same volume, which explains the strong range of some premium models. The trade-off is a higher raw-material cost and less tolerance to strong heat without careful pack cooling. Manufacturers compensate with liquid cooling loops and battery management software that limits charging speed once the cells warm up, which is one reason fast-charging curves vary so much between NMC models.

Neither chemistry is simply "better." A city car that spends its life on short errands and charges overnight benefits from LFP's durability. A car that regularly drives long motorway stretches, or tows a trailer, draws more on NMC's energy density. The badge on the boot rarely tells you which one sits underneath, so the only reliable source is the manufacturer's technical sheet for your exact version.

| Criteria | LFP | NMC |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Energy density | Lower | Higher |
| Manufacturing cost | Lower | Higher |
| Tolerance to 100% charging | Good, recommended regularly | Best kept for long trips |
| Heat sensitivity | Low | Higher |
| Typical cycle count | High | Solid with good cooling |

## Charging, where the difference is felt

**An LFP battery charges to 100% without risk; an NMC wears out faster if it stays full or empty too often.**

Day to day, LFP is more forgiving. Several manufacturers suggest charging it to 100% once a week or so, so the range estimate stays accurate. You can charge it fully without worrying about premature wear, despite a persistent myth inherited from NMC packs.

With an NMC, the cautious habit is to stay around 20 to 80% for everyday trips and keep full charges for long journeys. It is not an absolute rule, but it extends the battery's useful life over several years. An NMC charged to 100% twice a week doesn't tell the same story as one plugged in fully every single night.

The charging point itself matters too. A standard household socket handles either chemistry, but an old or poorly maintained home circuit heats up fast under a long, repeated charge. Have the circuit checked before you commit to charging every evening, especially in an older property, and consider a dedicated wallbox if you plan to charge daily regardless of chemistry.

## Price, range and trade-offs at purchase

LFP is usually cheaper to make. That cost sometimes shows in the price of entry-level versions, a point worth weighing against a comparable [plug-in or mild hybrid](/light-hybrid-or-plug-in-hybrid-real-difference/). NMC costs more and makes sense when range matters more than budget, for drivers who cover a lot of motorway miles or tow regularly.

Do not compare range figures alone. Look at the price of the exact version you want and the way you will really use it. A smaller battery used well can suit a commute of a few dozen kilometres, the kind of daily pattern where a mild hybrid or a full hybrid also makes sense if you're not ready to go fully electric yet.

Resale value plays a part too. A car with an LFP battery and a well-documented charging history tends to reassure a buyer who plans daily full charges, while an NMC car with a careful partial-charging record appeals to someone chasing maximum range. Ask the seller which chemistry sits in the car before you negotiate, since the figure rarely appears on the windscreen sticker.

## Lifespan, warranty and pack care

Both chemistries age, but not at the same pace or in the same way. What matters is the battery's **state of health**, meaning its remaining capacity, and the manufacturer warranty that covers it. Ask for the exact figure and the warranty duration before signing, whether it's a new car or a used one.

A workshop tip: avoid leaving the car in extreme heat or fully empty for long periods. Both extremes wear a battery faster than the chemistry itself. A regular technical check also helps catch abnormal pack ageing before it turns into a costly repair.

Before buying used, always ask for the battery's state-of-health reading and its full charging history if the previous owner kept one. A pack that spent years parked outdoors in full sun, or regularly left near empty, will show more wear than the mileage alone suggests, whatever the chemistry.

## Frequently asked questions

### Can you choose between LFP and NMC when buying?

Rarely. The chemistry depends on the model and version. Some manufacturers offer both depending on trim level, so check the spec sheet of the exact version before comparing prices.

### Should you charge an LFP battery to 100%?

Manufacturers using LFP often recommend an occasional full charge to recalibrate the display. For daily use, partial charging is perfectly fine.

### Does an NMC battery wear out faster than LFP?

Not necessarily. A well-cooled NMC used within a reasonable range ages properly. Habits such as heat exposure, repeated fast charging and deep discharges often weigh more than the chemistry itself.

### Which chemistry suits intensive fleet use?

LFP generally suits intensive use with full daily charges better, thanks to its higher tolerance to repeated cycles and its lower replacement cost.

### Does LFP handle cold weather as well as NMC?

Both chemistries lose some performance in cold weather. That's a general trait of lithium batteries, not something specific to either chemistry.

### Is an LFP battery heavier than an NMC for the same range?

Yes. To deliver the same range, an LFP pack weighs more than an NMC pack, because of its lower energy density for a given volume.
