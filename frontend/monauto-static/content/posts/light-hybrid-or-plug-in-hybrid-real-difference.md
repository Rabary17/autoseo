Walk into a garage for long enough and you see hybrids that do not match what their owners expected. Some believed they had bought an electric car because a small green motor appeared on the dashboard. Others plugged a PHEV into a home socket without realising it was still a hybrid. The word covers three distinct technologies, and each one changes your costs.

TL;DR: the mild hybrid never drives on electric power alone. The full hybrid and the plug-in hybrid can. The three essentials:

-   Mild hybrid: battery recharged only through braking, no socket.
-   Full hybrid: electric driving over short distances at low speed, no plug.
-   Plug-in hybrid (PHEV): larger battery, mains charging, higher purchase price.

## Three architectures under the bonnet

A mild hybrid works mainly as a petrol car. A small electric motor, fed by a low-voltage battery, assists the engine during acceleration. It can also shut the engine off when the car stops. It never moves the car alone: the petrol engine always provides the traction.

A full hybrid switches continuously between petrol and electric power, depending on what the driver asks. It can move on its electric motor alone over short distances and at low speed. Its battery recharges itself while driving, through energy recovery and the combustion engine.

A plug-in hybrid adds a larger battery that you charge from the mains. It drives electrically for a limited distance, then the petrol engine takes over. On paper it combines both approaches. In practice, its value depends almost entirely on how often you charge it.

## Charging is where the difference shows

Charging is the simplest criterion to check. A mild hybrid and a full hybrid do not plug in, so you do not need a socket at home for them. A PHEV does need regular charging. Without it, the car carries a heavy battery that does little useful work, and its advantage fades quickly.

Another point is often overlooked: on a standard household socket, a PHEV takes hours to charge. A dedicated wallbox speeds things up, but it only makes sense if you return home every evening to a space where you can install one.

## Real-world use: which trip suits which car

The mild hybrid suits short urban trips. It cuts fuel consumption and CO2 emissions without asking you to change your habits, which makes it a sensible choice for stop-and-go driving in town.

The full hybrid performs well on longer journeys, provided you do not expect a real electric range from it. It benefits most from energy recovery in town and on hilly roads.

The PHEV suits drivers who cover fewer kilometres each day than its electric range and can charge in the evening. For everyone else, it becomes a heavier and more expensive petrol car.

## Maintenance and repairs: what the garage sees

In the workshop, these hybrids are less alike than people think. The mild hybrid keeps a conventional petrol engine, with its oil changes, filters and timing belt. It adds a low-voltage battery and a small electrical system to watch.

The full hybrid and the PHEV carry a high-voltage battery. It should not be handled without suitable equipment and training. A diagnosis of its cells belongs in an authorised workshop, and a garage without training will not carry out the job. Before buying, ask for the battery’s service history and the manufacturer warranty that covers it.

## Price and running costs

The mild hybrid is generally the cheapest of the three. The PHEV costs more to buy, and its tax advantages depend on the country, according to Autojm. Do not compare headline prices alone: compare the extra cost upfront with what you will really save on your usual journeys.

### Is a plug-in hybrid worth it for city driving without a socket?

Usually not. Without regular charging, its battery stays a dead weight, and a mild hybrid costs less for the same urban trips.

### Can a mild hybrid drive on electric power alone?

No. The electric motor assists the petrol engine during acceleration and never moves the car by itself.

### What is the difference between a full hybrid and a PHEV?

A full hybrid recharges while driving and can run electrically over short distances. A PHEV charges from the mains, has a larger battery and offers a longer electric range, at a higher price.
