***A mild hybrid never plugs in, while a plug-in hybrid can drive several kilometres without a drop of petrol. Behind these two labels sit two different philosophies, two maintenance routines and two very different budgets day to day.***

Spend long enough in a garage and you see hybrids that don't match what their owners expected. Some believed they had bought an electric car because a small green motor appeared on the dashboard. Others plugged a PHEV into a home socket without realising it was still a hybrid. The word covers three distinct technologies. Here is what separates them, and what each one changes for your wallet.

TL;DR: the mild hybrid never drives on electric power alone, the full hybrid and the plug-in hybrid can. The three essentials:

-   **Mild hybrid**: battery recharged only through braking, no socket.
-   **Full hybrid**: electric driving over short distances at low speed, no mains charging.
-   **Plug-in hybrid (PHEV)**: larger battery, mains charging, higher purchase price.

## Three architectures under the bonnet

A mild hybrid works mainly as a petrol car. A small electric motor, fed by a low-voltage battery, assists the engine during acceleration. It can also shut the engine off when the car stops. It never moves the car alone: the petrol engine always provides the traction.

A full hybrid switches continuously between petrol and electric power, depending on what the driver asks. It can move on its electric motor alone over short distances and at low speed. Its battery recharges itself while driving, through energy recovery and the combustion engine.

A plug-in hybrid adds a noticeably larger battery that you charge from the mains. It drives electrically for a limited distance, then the petrol engine takes over. On paper it combines both approaches. In practice, its value depends almost entirely on how often you actually charge it. A PHEV charged twice a week tells a very different story from one plugged in every evening.

## Charging is where the difference shows

**A mild hybrid and a full hybrid never need a home socket. A PHEV does, and without one it carries a heavy, mostly useless battery.**

Charging is the simplest criterion to check. A mild hybrid and a full hybrid do not plug in, so you don't need a socket at home for either. A PHEV does need regular charging. Without it, the car carries a heavy battery that does little useful work, and its advantage fades fast.

Another point often overlooked: on a standard household socket, a PHEV takes hours to charge. A dedicated wallbox speeds things up, but it only makes sense if you return home every evening to a space where you can install one. If you park in a space with no socket at all, a plug-in hybrid stops making sense and a full hybrid suits you better.

One last detail people forget: the socket itself. An ordinary household outlet can handle charging, but an old or poorly maintained circuit heats up fast under a long, repeated load. Have the circuit checked before committing to nightly charging, especially in an older property.

## Real-world use: which trip suits which car

The mild hybrid suits short urban trips. It cuts fuel consumption and CO2 emissions without asking you to change your habits, which makes it a sensible choice for stop-and-go driving in town.

On the open road at a steady speed, the petrol engine does most of the work regardless of which hybrid you drive. Expect consumption close to a conventional petrol car in that scenario, and don't base your choice on it.

The full hybrid performs well on longer journeys, provided you don't expect a real electric range from it. It benefits most from energy recovery in town and on hilly roads.

The PHEV suits drivers who cover fewer kilometres each day than its electric range and can charge in the evening. For everyone else, it becomes a heavier and more expensive petrol car.

## Maintenance and repairs: what the garage sees

In the workshop, these hybrids are less alike than people think. The mild hybrid keeps a conventional petrol engine, with its oil changes, filters and timing belt. It adds a low-voltage battery and a small electrical system to watch, nothing more exotic than modern electronics.

The full hybrid and the PHEV carry a high-voltage battery. It should not be handled without suitable equipment and training. A diagnosis of its cells belongs in an authorised workshop, and an untrained garage won't take the job on. Before buying, ask for the battery's service history and the manufacturer warranty that covers it. The cell chemistry also shapes its longevity: our guide to [LFP and NMC batteries](/lfp-or-nmc-battery-what-changes/) explains what that technical choice really changes.

Regenerative braking also eases wear on pads and discs, since a hybrid relies less on its mechanical brakes. That said, discs that rarely work hard can rust and seize. Have them checked at every service, even if the pads still look new. ## Price and running costs

The mild hybrid is generally the cheapest of the three to buy. The PHEV costs the most, and its tax advantages depend heavily on the country and the scheme in force at the time of purchase. Don't compare sticker prices alone: compare the extra upfront cost with what you will really save on your usual journeys. A calculation based on your own numbers carries more weight than any sales pitch. A large price gap is only worth it if the savings genuinely follow.

Do the maths on your own routine. How many kilometres do you drive each day? How many charges can you realistically manage each week? What price gap separates the two models in your range? The answers matter more than any spec sheet. Resale also plays a part: a PHEV coming off warranty mainly appeals to buyers with a socket at home, which narrows the pool of interested buyers. A mild hybrid resells on the same criteria as a conventional petrol car.

## Which hybrid to choose without getting it wrong

Start from your actual driving pattern. If you drive mostly in town over short distances, the mild hybrid is enough and costs less. If you want to cut fuel costs on long trips without plugging in, the full hybrid is the simplest option. If you have a socket available every evening, the PHEV can be worth it.

Then check three things before signing: the battery warranty, its service history, and whether a trained workshop sits nearby. A well-chosen hybrid drives without surprises for years. A poor choice costs you at every fill-up and every invoice.

For a first hybrid, start with the simplest version. The mild hybrid suits anyone who wants a gain without changing their habits. The full hybrid appeals to drivers who stay mostly in town with no socket available. The plug-in hybrid is a choice of conviction, once the socket is there and the trips fit the range.

## Frequently asked questions

### Can a mild hybrid drive on electric power alone?

No. The electric motor assists the petrol engine during acceleration and never moves the car by itself. Its battery recharges only through energy recovery under braking.

### Does a plug-in hybrid charge on a standard household socket?

Yes, on a domestic socket. Charging stays slow, often several hours. A wallbox speeds up the process, especially if you return home every evening.

### What is the difference between a full hybrid and a PHEV?

A full hybrid recharges while driving and runs electrically over short distances. A PHEV charges from the mains, has a larger battery and offers a longer electric range, at a higher price.

### Is a plug-in hybrid worth it for city driving without a socket?

Usually not. Without regular charging, it carries a heavy, unused battery. A mild hybrid costs less to buy and covers the same urban trips.

### Does every hybrid offer the same level of driving assistance?

Not necessarily. The level of driver-assistance equipment depends on the model and trim, not on the hybrid technology itself.
