A new motorhome loses an average of **20% of its value in the first year**. On a €60,000 model, that’s around €12,000. Why is this depreciation steeper than for a car? Demand for used models is soaring. What’s more, the specific maintenance costs (gas, heating, insulation) put off potential buyers.

For a 400 km weekend trip with two bikes on the back, this loss becomes very real, very fast. A model bought for €60,000 in 2025 will only be worth €48,000 a year later. Even with just 5,000 km on the clock, the depreciation is unavoidable.

## Why does a new motorhome depreciate so quickly?

The [Argus rating](/en/motorhomes-campervans/buying-guide), created in 1927, estimates this loss of value. It combines the list price, mileage and vehicle condition. This benchmark reveals a harsh reality: as soon as it leaves the dealership, depreciation hits 15 to 20%. Several factors explain this.

-   **Used models are more appealing**: Buyers often prefer a recent but already run-in model. It’s cheaper and just as well equipped.
-   **Maintenance is costly**: Servicing the gas system, heating and insulation—these specific costs add up. As a result, they reduce the perceived value.
-   **Fittings wear out quickly**: Kitchen, bedding, storage—these interior features lose their shine faster than the bodywork.

For a couple planning a European tour in 2027, this depreciation isn’t just an abstract figure. A model bought for €55,000 in 2026 and sold for €40,000 in 2028 will cost around €7,500 a year. This calculation doesn’t include fuel or maintenance.

## How does depreciation change after the first year?

![Family preparing a motorhome for a spring road trip](/images/articles/decote-camping-car-neuf-1.webp)

After the first year, depreciation slows down—but it’s still significant. Here’s what the market data shows.

| Year | Average depreciation | Residual value (e.g. €60,000) |
| --- | --- | --- |
| 1st year | 20% | €48,000 |
| 2nd year | 10-15% | €40,800 – €43,200 |
| 3rd year | 8-12% | €35,900 – €39,700 |
| 5 years and over | 5-8% per year | €25,000 – €30,000 |

*Source: L’Argus, CapCar*

This curve shows that the sharpest loss happens in the first year. After that, depreciation settles at around 10% a year. However, it slows down after five years.

For a family road trip, this means a model bought new in 2026 will be hard to sell. It won’t exceed 50% of its original value before 2030.

## How can you limit the depreciation of your new motorhome?

While depreciation is inevitable, a few tips can help reduce it. Here are the main ones.

-   **Choose a sought-after model**: Low-profile or integrated models, which are more spacious and better insulated, depreciate more slowly than over-cab models. A [comparison with other types of motorhomes](/en/motorhomes-campervans/choosing-motorhome-type-guide) can help you decide.
-   **Keep mileage low**: Less than 10,000 km a year helps preserve value. For short trips, a used model that’s already run in makes more sense.
-   **Maintain it regularly**: A full service history reassures buyers. Invoices for servicing (gas, heating, tyres) are essential.
-   **Avoid unnecessary options**: High-end fittings (solar panels, luxury kitchen) don’t always pay off. It’s better to stick with standardised options.
-   **Sell at the right time**: Spring, before the peak season, is ideal. Buyers are more numerous and willing to pay a bit more.

For a couple planning a European tour in 2027, these tips can help better anticipate the budget. For example, a model bought for €55,000 in 2026 and sold for €40,000 in 2028 will cost around €7,500 a year. This amount doesn’t include fuel or maintenance.

## Should you buy new or used?

The choice depends on your budget and priorities. Here’s what tips the balance in favour of one or the other.

| Criteria | New | Used (2-3 years old) |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Purchase price | High (immediate depreciation) | 20-30% cheaper |
| Warranty | 2 to 5 years (manufacturer) | 6 months to 2 years (trade-in) |
| Equipment | Latest technology | May lack recent options |
| Maintenance | Controlled costs (warranty) | Risk of unexpected expenses |
| Future depreciation | High in the first year | Less pronounced |

*Source: FFCC, Wikicampers*

For a van conversion travel project, a used model can be a good compromise. A 2023 model bought for €45,000 in 2026 will already have taken its biggest depreciation hit. What’s more, it will cost less to insure. According to [cheaper motorhome insurance](/en/motorhomes-campervans), the price ranges from €200 to €400 a year.

## Mistakes to avoid to preserve your motorhome’s value

![Mechanic checking a motorhome engine during winterisation](/images/articles/decote-camping-car-neuf-2.webp)

Some practices speed up depreciation and make resale harder. Here are the main mistakes to avoid.

-   **Neglecting winterisation**: A motorhome that’s not properly prepared for winter loses value quickly. Key steps? Draining all water, switching off the electricity and cleaning the exterior ([Hymer](/en/motorhomes-campervans)).
-   **Forgetting to update the registration certificate**: After installing an E85 conversion kit, the fuel type must be changed to “FE”. Without this update, the vehicle could fail its _contrôle technique_ (MOT).
-   **Parking anywhere**: A motorhome can park like a car. However, “camping” (putting out an awning, setting up chairs) is banned on public roads. By-laws that specifically ban motorhomes are illegal—but it’s still best to check local rules.
-   **Ignoring accessory insurance**: A bike rack or the bikes themselves aren’t always covered by standard car insurance. An extension to your policy is often needed (MAAF, MAIF).

For a family road trip with two children, these details make all the difference. A well-maintained motorhome that complies with the rules holds its value better. It also avoids nasty surprises—like a fine for illegal parking or an insurance claim being rejected.
