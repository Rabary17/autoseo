***A new motorhome typically loses 15 to 20% of its value in the first year alone. On a €60,000 model, that's €9,000 to €12,000 gone before the first winterisation.***

Why does this depreciation run steeper than a car's? The used market stays in strong demand, and buyers stay cautious about the vehicle's specific upkeep costs: gas, heating, insulation. For a 400 km weekend with two bikes on the back, that loss becomes very real, very fast, and it's worth planning for before you sign at the dealership. Here's what the market data shows, and the levers that help limit this loss of value.

TL;DR: the first year carries most of the depreciation, which then slows down markedly.

-   Average depreciation seen in the **first year**: 15% to 20% of the purchase price.
-   Depreciation slows down clearly from the **second year**, to around 8% to 15% a year.
-   Low-profile and coachbuilt models generally resell better than entry-level over-cab models.

## Why does a new motorhome depreciate so fast?

**Direct answer**: depreciation runs steeper than a car's because the used market stays very active, and buyers stay cautious about the specific upkeep costs: gas, heating and waterproofing.

Several reasons explain this fast depreciation, starting the moment it leaves the dealership:

-   **Used models appeal more.** Many buyers prefer a recent model that's already run in, cheaper to buy and just as well equipped as a new one.
-   **Upkeep weighs on perceived value.** Servicing the gas system, the heating and the waterproofing are specific costs that add to a motorhome's budget compared with an ordinary car.
-   **Interior fittings wear out fast.** The kitchen, bedding and storage lose their "new" look faster than the bodywork itself.

For a couple planning a European tour a few years out, this depreciation isn't just an abstract figure. A model bought for €55,000 and resold two years later around €42,000 works out to roughly €6,500 a year in usage cost, excluding fuel and routine upkeep.

## How does depreciation change after the first year?

After the first year, depreciation slows down clearly, but stays significant in the following years.

| Period | Depreciation seen | Approximate residual value (base €60,000) |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Year 1 | 15% to 20% | €48,000 to €51,000 |
| Year 2 | 8% to 15% | €41,000 to €47,000 |
| Year 3 | 8% to 12% | €36,000 to €43,000 |
| Beyond 5 years | 5% to 8% a year | Varies with upkeep |

*Market order-of-magnitude figures, for guidance only. The vehicle's upkeep and exact model shift these figures noticeably from one example to another.*

This curve shows that the sharpest loss hits in the first year. For a model bought new, it's hard to hope for keeping more than half its original value beyond four to five years of ownership.

## How can you limit your new motorhome's depreciation?

While depreciation stays inevitable, a few levers help reduce its scale.

-   **Choose a sought-after model.** Low-profile or coachbuilt motorhomes, more spacious and better insulated, generally depreciate slower than entry-level over-cab models. Our [comparison of low-profile, coachbuilt and over-cab motorhomes](/choosing-motorhome-type-guide/) helps guide that choice.
-   **Keep annual mileage down.** Below 10,000 km a year, value holds up better. For short, frequent trips, an already run-in used model often makes more sense anyway.
-   **Maintain it regularly.** A complete service record, with invoices for gas, heating and tyre work, strongly reassures buyers at resale.
-   **Avoid unnecessary options.** High-end fittings don't always pay for themselves at resale. Standard, sought-after options tend to perform better.
-   **Sell at the right time.** Spring, just before peak season, usually brings the strongest buyer demand.

## Should you buy new or used?

The choice mostly depends on your budget and how you plan to use the vehicle.

| Criteria | New | Used (2–3 years old) |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Purchase price | High, immediate depreciation in year one | Generally 20% to 30% cheaper |
| Warranty | Full manufacturer warranty | Limited warranty, depends on the seller |
| Equipment | Latest technology available | May lack recent options |
| Upkeep | Controlled costs under warranty | Risk of unexpected costs to plan for |
| Future depreciation | High in the first year | Noticeably less pronounced |

For a long-haul travel project, a used model can be a good compromise: a motorhome already 2 to 3 years old has generally absorbed its sharpest depreciation, and often costs less to insure. For narrowing down your search on the used market, our [used motorhome buyer's guide](/used-motorhome-buying-guide/) covers prices and pitfalls to avoid.

Don't forget to factor in running costs beyond the purchase price itself. Tolls alone can add up fast on a European tour: our guide to [Class 2 motorhome tolls](/motorhome-class-2-toll-guide/) breaks down the rates by country, a budget line that's easy to underestimate when you're focused on the sticker price.

## Mistakes to avoid to preserve your motorhome's value

![Mechanic checking a motorhome engine during winterisation](/images/articles/decote-camping-car-neuf-2.webp)

Some habits speed up depreciation and make resale harder.

-   **Neglecting winterisation.** A motorhome poorly prepared for winter loses value fast. Our [full winterisation checklist](/motorhome-winterisation-checklist/) covers the steps not to skip: draining the water system, cutting the power, protecting the exterior.
-   **Forgetting to update the registration certificate** after a conversion, for example to E85 fuel. Without that update, the vehicle risks failing its roadworthiness test.
-   **Neglecting waterproofing.** An untreated leak damages the structure long-term and drops resale value far faster than a cosmetic flaw. Our guide to [motorhome waterproofing checks](/motorhome-waterproofing-check-guide/) covers what to check every year.
-   **Parking anywhere.** A motorhome can park like a car, but "camping" on the public road, putting out an awning, setting up chairs, stays banned. A blanket, permanent ban targeting motorhomes specifically is generally unlawful; only targeted, justified restrictions hold up. Our article on [parking rules in towns](/motorhome-parking-city-rules/) covers that distinction.
-   **Ignoring accessory insurance.** A bike rack or the bikes themselves aren't always covered by a standard policy; an extension with your insurer is often needed.

For a family road trip, these details make all the difference. A well-maintained motorhome that follows the rules holds its value better, and avoids nasty surprises at resale and at the roadworthiness test alike.

## Frequently asked questions

### How much does a new motorhome depreciate in the first year?

Generally between 15% and 20% of its purchase price, depending on the model and how sought-after it is on the used market.

### Does depreciation slow down after the first year?

Yes, it generally drops to 8% to 15% a year in the second and third years, before settling around 5% to 8% a year beyond five years.

### Is it better to buy new or used to limit the loss of value?

A motorhome 2 to 3 years old has often already absorbed its sharpest depreciation, a sound choice for anyone who prioritises total ownership cost over the latest technology.

### Which type of motorhome depreciates the slowest?

Low-profile and coachbuilt models, more sought-after at resale, generally depreciate slower than entry-level over-cab models.

### Does poor upkeep significantly affect depreciation?

Yes, especially neglected waterproofing. An untreated leak damages the structure and drops resale value far faster than a simple cosmetic flaw.

### Can a town hall completely ban motorhome parking?

No, a blanket, permanent ban targeting motorhomes specifically is generally unlawful. Only targeted, justified restrictions, for example overnight or seasonal ones, hold up.
