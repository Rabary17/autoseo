***Winter is coming, with the risk of frost, mould and a flat battery. For a motorhome stored until spring, a precise checklist prevents a nasty surprise when it's time to start it up again.***

TL;DR: drain every water system without exception, protect the battery and gas cylinders from the cold, then clean the exterior to limit corrosion. Here are the 12 steps to follow, in order, for a risk-free winterisation.

-   A single **residual drop of water** is enough to crack a tank or a pipe once it freezes.
-   The **battery** should be disconnected or removed, and ideally placed on a trickle charger.
-   A **final walk-around inspection** before putting the vehicle away for good catches any step you may have missed.

## 1. Drain every water system without exception

This is the most critical step. A single drop of residual water can freeze and crack a tank or a pipe. Open the water heater's drain valve, often located under the vehicle and marked with a red or yellow cap. Let the water flow until not a drop remains.

Don't forget the grey water. Flip the drain lever on the tank under the chassis. Let it empty until the pipe runs dry. Finally, open every tap, hot and cold, and run the water pump for 10 seconds to purge the pipes. A low-pressure compressor (max 2 bar) can clear the last drops. **Avoid**: leaving even a small amount of water in the tanks, however little.

## 2. Cut the power supply and isolate the battery

Unplug the motorhome from the mains and switch off the main circuit breaker. For the battery, you have two options: remove it and store it somewhere dry and frost-free, ideally on a trickle charger, or leave it in place but disconnected, taking off the negative terminal first, then the positive.

Even light frost can damage the cells. If you store the battery in an unheated garage, wrap it in an insulating blanket and set it on a wooden board to avoid direct contact with the cold floor. Check the electrolyte level if it's a lead-acid battery, topping up with distilled water if needed, and clean the terminals with a wire brush to prevent corrosion. **Avoid**: leaving the battery connected with no supervision, which can cause a full discharge and lasting damage.

## 3. Store gas cylinders safely

Close the valves on the gas cylinders and disconnect them from the system. If your motorhome stays outdoors, remove them and store them in a ventilated space, such as a cellar or a garden shed. A dedicated frost-free locker also works. In France, regulations ban storing gas cylinders in a living space or a garage attached to a home. Our guide to [gas safety in a motorhome](/motorhome-gas-safety-guide/) covers the equipment and habits to check every season.

If you leave the cylinders in the vehicle, make sure the gas locker stays well ventilated. Clear the vents, and secure the cylinders firmly so they can't shift in bad weather.

## 4. Clean the exterior to prevent corrosion

A dirty motorhome left standing for months sees its seals and bodywork deteriorate faster. Wash it with a pressure washer, removing mud, insect residue and salt, especially if you've driven near the coast. Pay attention to the wheel arches, the underbody and any area where water pools.

Dry it thoroughly with a chamois or a microfibre cloth, then apply a protective wax to the bodywork to limit the effects of frost and damp. Check the condition of the door and window seals: a porous seal lets in moisture and encourages mould. If needed, apply a seal-specific product, such as a silicone spray. **Avoid**: skipping the clean. Mud and salt speed up corrosion.

## 5. Protect the interior from mould and rodents

Open every cupboard, drawer and door to air out the living space. Vacuum to remove crumbs and food residue, which attract rodents. Place silica gel sachets or moisture absorbers in sensitive spots, such as under the sink or near the windows.

To deter mice, natural repellents work well: peppermint essential oil on cotton wool, or mothballs. Mechanical traps, without poison, avoid lingering smells. Leave mattresses and cushions standing upright to help airflow and prevent damp patches. **Avoid**: leaving food in the cupboards, even small amounts. It attracts rodents and can grow mould.

## 6. Check fluid levels and condition

Even in winter storage, a motorhome needs its fluids in good shape. Check the engine oil level and top up if needed with oil suited to your engine, generally 5W-30 or 5W-40 for a diesel. Check the coolant, which should sit between the "min" and "max" marks on the expansion tank.

If the level is low, add a water and antifreeze mix, following the proportions on the bottle. Finally, check the brake fluid and the windscreen washer level. An empty washer reservoir can freeze and crack. A low brake fluid level can point to a leak or worn pads.

## 7. Inflate the tyres and protect the wheels

Underinflated tyres wear out faster and can deform under the vehicle's weight. Inflate them to the pressure recommended by the manufacturer, usually shown on a sticker inside the driver's door or in the owner's manual, adding 0.2 bar to offset the pressure drop in cold weather.

If possible, raise the motorhome slightly on axle stands or chocks to relieve the tyres and prevent flat spots. If you can't raise the vehicle, move it a few centimetres every two months instead. Protect the rims and tyres with a wheel cover or a protective film to limit UV and moisture damage. **Avoid**: leaving the tyres underinflated, which speeds up wear and raises the risk of deformation.

## 8. Cover the vehicle to protect it from the weather

A suitable cover protects the bodywork from UV, rain and snow. Choose a breathable cover, polypropylene for example, to prevent condensation building up underneath it, and make sure it's securely fastened to withstand wind. Avoid a non-breathable plastic cover, which traps moisture and encourages corrosion.

If you store your motorhome outdoors, pick a spot sheltered from the prevailing wind and from trees, since leaves and sap can damage the paintwork. Check regularly that the cover hasn't shifted and that water isn't pooling on it. **Avoid**: a non-breathable cover, which traps moisture and encourages corrosion.

## 9. Check the roof and opening seals for leaks

A leaking roof or a poorly sealed window can cause serious damage over winter. Visually inspect the roof, looking for cracks, peeling seals or damp patches. If you spot an issue, apply a motorhome-specific silicone sealant, designed to resist UV and bad weather.

Also check the door and window seals: a damaged seal should be replaced before winter storage. Inspect the skylights and vents, cleaning them and making sure they close properly, to keep out water or snow.

## 10. Protect the engine and the mechanicals

If your motorhome stays idle for several months, the engine can suffer. To avoid starting problems come spring, run it for 10 to 15 minutes every two months, ideally driving a few kilometres to bring it up to temperature. If you can't drive it, let it idle, but avoid long idling sessions, which cause carbon build-up.

Also check the timing belt. If it's near its wear limit, generally between 100,000 and 150,000 km, replace it before winter storage to prevent it failing on restart. Finally, lubricate the locks and hinges so they don't freeze or seize up. **Avoid**: leaving the engine idle for months, which can cause carbon build-up or seizure.

This quiet period is also a good time to plan ahead: a roadworthiness test due in spring is often easier to book outside peak season, and it's a good moment to compare motorhome insurance options before your policy renews.

## 11. Prepare the interior equipment

Unplug and store every electrical appliance. Coffee maker, kettle or portable heater: everything should be unplugged to avoid a short circuit or overheating. Empty and clean the fridge, leaving its door open to prevent odours.

If your motorhome has gas heating, purge the gas lines and check the burner's condition. Finally, remove perishable food from the cupboards and store cutlery and crockery in airtight containers to keep rodents away.

## 12. Do a final walk-around before storage

### Final checklist

Before putting your motorhome away for good, do one last walk-around. Here's a quick checklist to make sure nothing's forgotten:

-   Every tap is closed and the tanks are drained.
-   The battery is disconnected or removed and stored somewhere dry.
-   The gas cylinders are closed and stored safely.
-   The vehicle is clean, inside and out.
-   Oil, coolant and brake fluid levels are correct.
-   The tyres are inflated to the right pressure.
-   The cover is securely fastened and breathable.
-   The seals and waterproofing are in good condition.
-   The engine has been run recently or will be started regularly.
-   The electrical appliances are unplugged and stored.

Once this checklist is done, your motorhome is ready for winter. Come spring, a simple fluid check and a battery recharge will be enough to hit the road with peace of mind. ## Frequently asked questions

### Do you need to drain the water even for a few weeks of winter storage?

Yes, as soon as the vehicle sits idle during a frost period, even briefly. A single night of frost is enough to crack a tank or a pipe holding residual water.

### Can you leave the battery connected all winter with no supervision?

No, that's not recommended. Disconnect it or remove it, and ideally place it on a trickle charger to avoid a full discharge that would damage it permanently.

### How often should you run the engine during winterisation?

Roughly every two months, for 10 to 15 minutes, ideally driving a few kilometres to bring it up to temperature. Avoid long idling sessions, which cause carbon build-up.

### Does an ordinary plastic tarpaulin work to cover a motorhome in winter?

No, avoid a non-breathable cover. It traps moisture underneath and encourages corrosion. Choose a breathable cover instead, polypropylene for example.

### What should you do if you find a leak or a crack during winterisation?

Have the tank or pipe repaired before storing the vehicle. An untreated fault usually gets worse while the vehicle sits idle over winter, and costs more to fix once spring comes.
