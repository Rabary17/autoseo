***On a product page, "self-driving" can mean almost anything. On the road, it comes down to one simple point: who is watching the road, you or the system?***

The **SAE J3016** standard answers that question with six levels, from level 0 to level 5. It's the classification carmakers, regulators and insurers actually rely on, whatever the brochure calls the feature.

TL;DR: most cars sold today sit at level 2, where the driver stays responsible at all times. Level 3 changes the picture within precise conditions, but it stays rare and tightly regulated.

-   **Levels 0 and 1**: the driver does everything, or nearly everything.
-   **Level 2**: the system handles two functions at once, the driver keeps watching throughout.
-   **Level 3**: the system drives within a defined framework, and the driver must be able to take back control.
-   No vehicle sold to private buyers in France reaches **level 4 or 5** today.

## The six SAE levels in brief

| Level | Who's actually driving | Typical example |
| --- | --- | --- |
| 0 | The driver, full stop | Warning alerts only |
| 1 | The driver, with one automated function | Adaptive cruise control, or lane keeping, never both together |
| 2 | The system handles two functions, driver supervises | Cruise control plus lane keeping |
| 3 | The system drives within a defined framework | Motorway driving at moderate speed |
| 4 | The system drives alone, within a limited area | Robotaxi on a mapped urban route |
| 5 | The system drives alone, everywhere, in any weather | Not commercially available |

Level 0 means a car with no driving aid beyond warnings. Level 1 adds a single automated function, like adaptive cruise control or lane keeping, never both at once. **Level 2 combines several functions**, for example cruise control and lane keeping together, but the driver must stay alert the whole time, hands ready to take over.

At level 3, the system takes over driving within defined conditions, for example on a motorway at moderate speed, and it can ask the driver to resume control with a stated delay. Levels 4 and 5 mean driving with no human intervention, within a limited area for level 4, everywhere and in any weather for level 5. No vehicle sold to private buyers in France reaches either of these two levels today.

This classification comes from SAE International, an American engineering body, and now serves as the global reference, picked up by European regulators including the GSR2 rule that governs mandatory safety equipment. For automatic emergency braking specifically, the regulatory framework changed in 2026, covered in our article on [automatic braking rules from 2026](/automatic-braking-what-rules-require-2026/).

## Level 2, today's market standard

Most cars sold today sit at level 2. Adaptive cruise control manages the distance to the vehicle ahead, and lane keeping nudges the trajectory back into place. **These aids work well on motorways and dual carriageways, but they never replace holding the wheel.**

The trap is well known to ergonomists and insurers alike. After watching the car drive itself for a few kilometres, attention drops. The standard stays clear on this point: a level 2 system remains the driver's full responsibility, even when the hands feel free and the wheel seems to steer itself through a wide bend.

A claim during level 2 use gets treated as an ordinary accident, with the driver held liable just as if no assistance had been active at all.

## Level 3, the real shift in responsibility

**Level 3 is the point where the system takes responsibility for driving, but only within a strictly defined framework.** Outside it, in town, on a secondary road or in bad weather, it simply stops working, and it can ask the driver to retake control with a delay stated in advance in the owner's manual.

Few models sold in France genuinely offer this level, and its use stays governed by each European country's own rules. Before relying on it, check the activation conditions described in the manual, along with what the manufacturer lists as your obligations while the system drives in your place.

How a level 3 claim gets handled can differ depending on whether the system was active within its intended conditions at the moment of the accident, which is exactly why reading the manual matters more here than at any other level.

## What the standard says vs what marketing says

Marketing often talks about self-driving where the standard talks about assistance. The difference matters for your safety, and for how an insurer treats a claim afterward. A flattering term on a brochure doesn't bind an insurer the same way a precise level stated in the vehicle's official technical sheet does.

This marketing haze isn't unique to one brand. **Read the declared SAE level and its list of usage conditions**, not the slogan on the page. A badge called "autopilot" or "semi-autonomous driving" carries no fixed meaning on its own: the same commercial name can sit at level 2 on one model and level 3 on another, depending on the version and the market.

## How to check your vehicle's actual level

Three simple checks keep you from relying on marketing alone. First, check the vehicle's **official technical sheet**, which rarely uses the word "level" but lists the active functions and their conditions of use. Second, read the manual's chapter on driving aids, which spells out the weather, road types and speeds where each function switches on.

Third, ask your insurer before treating any system as a given in the event of an accident. A claim during level 2 use is handled as an ordinary accident, with the driver responsible throughout. At level 3, treatment can vary depending on whether the system was active within its intended conditions at the time.

## Why this classification matters for your next purchase

When choosing a vehicle, the actual SAE level on offer deserves as much scrutiny as fuel consumption or boot space. Two models advertising the same pitch, "semi-autonomous driving" or "advanced driving assistant", can hide very different technical realities depending on the brand and the trim level.

Always ask for a test drive with the driving aids switched on, rather than trusting the technical sheet alone. A poorly calibrated level 2 system, one that reacts too late or too sharply in a bend, matters as much to your decision as its level on paper. Specialised comparison reviews usually cover this feel in detail, a useful complement to the manual before you commit to a purchase.

## Frequently asked questions

### What's the difference between level 2 and level 3?

At level 2, the driver watches the road at all times and stays responsible for driving. At level 3, the system drives within a defined framework, and the driver must be able to take back control when asked, with a stated delay.

### Can you take your hands off the wheel at level 2?

No. The driver must stay attentive and ready to intervene at any moment, even if the hands leave the wheel briefly thanks to a presence sensor.

### Who is liable in a level 3 accident?

It depends on national regulations and on the system's activation conditions at the time. This is a civil liability question, best checked with a professional or your insurer before any regular use.

### Does a level 5 vehicle already exist for sale in France?

No. No vehicle sold to private buyers in France reaches level 4 or level 5 of the SAE standard today. These levels stay experimental or limited to specific uses, like shuttles on a closed circuit.

### Does a term like "autopilot" correspond to a specific SAE level?

No. It's a commercial name, not an SAE classification. The same marketing name can sit at level 2 on one model and level 3 on another, depending on the version and the market. Always check the technical sheet rather than the name given to the feature.
