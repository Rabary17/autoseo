For a tradesperson who transports a 5-metre ladder and lightweight scaffolding every week, or a landscaper who occasionally moves tools, a van fitted with a side-mounted ladder carrier or roof rack frees up cargo space. The choice depends on weight, frequency of use, and legal constraints. Here’s what to consider before investing.

## 1\. Side-mounted ladder carrier or roof rack: which system for which use?

![Lockable side-mounted ladder carrier on a utility van, with an aluminium ladder loaded](/images/articles/galerie-et-porte-echelle-fourgon-1.webp)

Two solutions dominate the market, each meeting a specific need:

-   **Fixed side-mounted ladder carrier**: mounted on the side of the van, it supports 50 to 100 kg and locks with a key. Ideal for telescopic ladders or lightweight scaffolding. For example, the _Alu-Lift_ model from _Sortimo_ is fitted on 60% of tradespeople’s vans in France (source: _Kit Utilitaire_).
-   **Roof rack**: designed for heavy loads (up to 200 kg), it can carry roof boxes, solar panels, or long materials. Anodised aluminium models resist corrosion. However, a poorly fitted rack can vibrate at high speeds and damage the bodywork.
-   **Rear-mounted ladder carrier**: less common, it attaches to the tailgate. It suits short vans like the Renault Kangoo or Citroën Berlingo. Its advantage? It frees up the sides for side doors or adhesive advertising.

A roofer transporting tiles and battens will opt for a reinforced roof rack, sacrificing 10 cm of height under bridges. Conversely, an electrician who moves conduits twice a month will settle for a lockable side-mounted ladder carrier.

## 3\. Approval and regulations: what the law says

A ladder carrier or roof rack modifies the vehicle’s structure. Two points must be checked without fail:

-   **Gross vehicle weight rating (PTAC)**: a roof rack loaded with 200 kg on a Citroën Jumpy (PTAC 2.8 t) reduces the payload capacity. Exceeding the PTAC can result in a €135 fine and a fail at the _contrôle technique_ (MOT).
-   **Vehicle approval**: if the van has been converted in a workshop (bench seat, fixed kitchen), it may switch to the VASP category. As a result, the CTTE designation disappears from the registration certificate, replaced by VASP. This may disqualify the vehicle from van conversion grants (up to €9,000 in 2026).
-   **Insurance**: an undeclared ladder carrier or roof rack can lead to a claim being rejected in case of theft or accident. MAAF and MAIF require an extension of cover for accessories fitted outside the vehicle (source: _MAAF_).

For a van that remains CTTE, no action is needed if the equipment weighs less than 50 kg. Above that, a declaration at the _préfecture_ (local authority) is sufficient, without modifying the registration certificate. However, a van converted to VASP must go through the DREAL (Regional Directorate for the Environment, Planning and Housing) for approval.

## 4\. Installation: DIY or call in a professional?

![Reinforced roof rack on a utility van, loaded with a ladder and roof box](/images/articles/galerie-et-porte-echelle-fourgon-2.webp)

Fitting a side-mounted ladder carrier on a Renault Trafic takes 2 hours with a drill and a torque wrench. A roof rack, however, requires specific tools:

-   **Essential tools**: spirit level, torque wrench (tightening torque 25–30 Nm), metal drill bit, silicone sealant for waterproofing.
-   **Fixing points**: roof racks are fixed to roof gutters or side rails. On a Peugeot Boxer, the original anchor points support loads up to 150 kg. On an older Ford Transit, you’ll need to drill the bodywork and reinforce it with backing plates.
-   **Mistakes to avoid**: drilling too close to the edges (risk of corrosion), overtightening bolts (bodywork deformation), or forgetting to check rail alignment. The rack must be perfectly horizontal to distribute the load.

A certified installer charges between €150 and €300 to fit a roof rack. The advantage? They provide a certificate of compliance, useful for inspections or resale. For a ladder carrier, expect to pay €80–120 in labour.

## 5\. Maintenance and safety: extending your equipment’s lifespan

A poorly maintained roof rack or ladder carrier becomes a road hazard. Here’s what to do:

-   **Cleaning**: after each job, remove mud and cement residue with a low-pressure water jet. Abrasive products scratch anodised aluminium.
-   **Checking fixings**: every 5,000 km, retighten bolts and check the condition of rubber mounts. A loose bolt can cause the load to come off while driving.
-   **Winter protection**: before winter, lubricate moving parts (hinges, locks) with silicone grease. Painted steel racks don’t withstand road salt well. Apply a protective wax coating.
-   **Road safety**: a poorly secured ladder can come loose and hit another vehicle. Use ratchet straps (standard EN 12195-2) and check tension before each journey. On motorways, reduce your speed by 10 km/h to limit vibrations.

A well-maintained ladder carrier lasts 10 years. Anodised aluminium models resist corrosion better than painted steel, but cost 30% more to buy. For occasional use, a basic model is sufficient. However, for daily use, invest in top-of-the-range equipment.

## 6\. Alternatives: when a roof rack or ladder carrier isn’t enough

Some trades require more robust solutions. For example, a removal company transporting bulky furniture will need a tail lift. A roofer, on the other hand, will opt for a material trailer:

-   **Material trailer**: for very heavy loads (over 200 kg), a dedicated trailer prevents overloading the van. Note that a category B licence is sufficient up to 750 kg gross train weight (PTRA). Above that, a BE licence is required.
-   **Tail lift**: mounted at the rear of the van, it lifts loads up to 500 kg. Ideal for pallet deliveries or removals. Price: €3,000–6,000 including installation.
-   **Roof box**: watertight and lockable, it protects tools from the elements. Thule or Yakima models support 50–75 kg. Expect to pay €500–900 depending on size.
