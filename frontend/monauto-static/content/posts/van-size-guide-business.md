A 3m³ van carries a payload of 250 to 350 kg. It’s ideal for urban deliveries or tradesmen’s tools. The 6m³ version, meanwhile, handles 1,000–1,200 kg and fits under 2.10 m barriers. Perfect for removals or construction sites with bulky equipment. Here’s how to decide based on your routes, budget and tax rules.

## 1\. Dimensions and capacity: what each volume can actually carry

On paper, 3m³ seems modest. In practice, it’s the space of a single bed. It measures 1.90 m long, 1.40 m wide and 1.35 m high. That’s enough for 8 to 10 moving boxes, a pallet of materials or a compressor.

The 6m³ nearly doubles the length (2.80–3.10 m) and gains 20 cm in height (1.85 m vs 2.10 m). As a result, it can fit a washing machine upright or two bikes side by side.

For an electrician carrying cables and tools, the 3m³ is enough. However, a landscaper with ride-on mowers or a carpenter with 3 m panels will need the 6m³. The extra height avoids having to remove rear doors on models like the Citroën Jumpy or Renault Trafic.

## 2\. Practical uses: when the 3m³ is enough, when the 6m³ is essential

Three scenarios where the 3m³ remains the most cost-effective solution:

-   **Urban deliveries**: 5 to 10 parcels per round, short trips (under 50 km). A Renault Kangoo or Peugeot Partner fits everywhere, even in the narrow streets of Lyon or Bordeaux.
-   **Solo tradesmen**: a plumber or locksmith can carry their tools in organised cases. The smaller volume keeps fuel costs down (5.5 L/100 km on average, compared with 7.5 L/100 km for a 6m³).
-   **Service fleets**: companies that share several vehicles optimise their usage rate. For example, 3 x 3m³ vans for 5 technicians work better than one underused 6m³.

The 6m³ becomes essential in these cases:

-   **Removals**: a 30 m² studio flat fits in 6m³ (sofa, bed, table, 20 boxes). A 3m³ would require two trips.
-   **Sites with heavy equipment**: a cement mixer or mini excavator weighs over 800 kg. The 6m³ handles this weight without overloading.
-   **Furniture deliveries**: a corner sofa or fitted kitchen fits upright, without risk of scratches.

## 3\. Tax and total cost: the little-known advantage of CTTE vans

3m³ and 6m³ vans share a key tax advantage. The **CTTE** designation on the registration document (line J1) exempts them from the annual company vehicle tax (TVS), regardless of engine type. Thus, a Citroën Jumpy with a 1.6 BlueHDi 120 hp (7 CV) avoids this tax, unlike an MPV such as the Kia Carnival (up to €6,500/year if it exceeds 16 CV). The guide [Choisir un utilitaire](/en/commercial-vehicles-fleets/choosing-van) details these size considerations.

Other savings include:

-   100% VAT reclaimable on long-term leases (LLD) if used strictly for business.
\\n-   Cheaper insurance: a CTTE van costs 20–30% less to insure than a VASP (vehicle with living accommodation, such as a campervan), even for the same model.
-   Controlled maintenance: a full exhaust system (muffler + pipe) costs between €100 and €400, labour included. The Jumpy 1.6 BlueHDi scores 7.5/10 for reliability, with a head gasket risk only on the 165 hp versions (2016–2018).

## 4\. Conversion and legality: watch out for the VASP designation

A van converted into a mobile office or workshop must stay below the VASP threshold to keep the CTTE designation. Here are the criteria:

-   Fixed storage (bolted shelves, not removable crates).
-   Fixed table and seating (convertible bench into bed accepted).
-   Fixed kitchen (gas, electric or diesel, no portable stove).
-   Conversion not removable in under 2 hours.
-   Fixed bed (a mattress on planks is enough to trigger the obligation).

If your conversion is basic (e.g. a folding table and plastic crates), you remain CTTE. This avoids extra costs. However, a van converted into a VASP-approved campervan loses the TVS exemption. Its insurance then increases by 30–50%.

## 5\. Electric or diesel: which engine for which use?

2026 electric vans offer 250 to 460 km WLTP range. A Renault Kangoo E-Tech (3m³) manages 250 km, enough for a day’s city deliveries. A Mercedes eSprinter (6m³) reaches 440 km, but its price (from €65,000) limits it to fleets with dedicated charging points.

Diesel remains relevant for:

-   Long trips (over 300 km/day) without charging time.
-   Sites without electricity access (e.g. fibre installation in rural areas).
-   Tight budgets: a new diesel Jumpy costs €30,000, compared with €45,000 for its electric equivalent.

For mixed use (city + motorway), the plug-in hybrid (e.g. Ford Transit Custom PHEV) offers 50 km on full electric and a total range of 500 km.
