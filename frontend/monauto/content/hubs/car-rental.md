Four needs, four pricing models: a weekend getaway, landing at the airport and driving off straight away, signing a zero-deposit long-term hire agreement, or buying out an excess after an accident. This section groups these scenarios to direct you straight to the article that matches yours.

**Before comparing the four pages below, three key points to remember:**

-   Short-term hire (weekend, airport): charged by the day, often with a high but buyable excess.
-   Zero-deposit LLD: fixed monthly payment, higher rent than with a deposit, no upfront payment.
-   Excess and no-claims bonus have nothing in common: the first depends on the hire contract, the second follows your personal history as regulated by law.

## Short-term or long-term hire: how to decide based on your needs

Two approaches clash. Short-term hire covers a one-off need, from a few hours to around 90 days: a business trip, a weekend, a temporary replacement while your car’s in the garage. LLD (long-term hire) caters for regular use over several months or years, with a fixed monthly fee that often includes maintenance and breakdown cover. The choice rarely comes down to the price shown at any given moment, but to how often you’ll use the car over the year.

| Criteria | Short-term hire | Zero-deposit LLD |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Duration | A few hours to 90 days | 12 to 60 months |
| Payment | Daily/weekly rate + options | All-inclusive fixed monthly fee |
| Upfront payment | Deposit blocked on credit card | None, slightly higher monthly fee |
| Excess | €800 to €1,500 on average, buyable | Set in contract, often lower |
| Typical use | Weekend, trip, airport | Main household vehicle |

Beyond 15 to 20 days of hire per year, zero-deposit LLD almost always works out cheaper than a string of short-term contracts, despite a slightly higher monthly fee. Below that threshold, comparing several hire companies for a one-off need usually costs less: the bill shoots up as soon as you add options at the counter.

A student who goes home to see their parents two weekends a month rarely benefits from a 12-month LLD: the monthly fee doesn’t justify such occasional use. Conversely, a sales rep who drives daily for work quickly offsets the fixed fee, with maintenance and breakdown cover included. The most reliable calculation involves adding up the real annual cost of one-off hires, including options and excess, then comparing it to the total of twelve monthly payments.

The question also arises abroad. Hiring on the spot upon arrival often costs less than booking a package from France for a long stay, but it means checking the locally applied excess—sometimes significantly higher than in France—and whether the visited country accepts French licences.

## Cutting weekend costs: what really affects the price

Booking a Saturday for two days almost always costs more than booking Friday evening for three. Hire companies calculate their rates per full night, and a one-day gap often changes the price category displayed on screen.

![Collecting the keys to a hire car for a weekend](/images/articles/location-courte-longue-duree-1.webp)

Our page on hiring a car cheaply for a weekend details the best booking slots and tips that can cut the bill by 20 to 30% on a standard round trip. The return day matters just as much as the departure day: dropping the car off before noon often avoids being charged for an extra full day.

The included mileage also deserves a quick check before confirming the booking. A limit of 100 or 200 kilometres per day may seem generous, but a weekend trip to the countryside can easily exceed it without realising. Options added at the counter—GPS, child seat, additional driver—push the bill up by €5 to €15 per day each: booking them online in advance almost always costs less.

## Hiring at the airport without overpaying on your card

Counters in airport zones add a surcharge that can exceed 15% of the base rate, on top of local taxes specific to each hub. It’s not inevitable: our article airport car hire tips lists ways to book off-site while keeping the convenience of pick-up on arrival, including shuttle service.

A few hundred metres’ difference between an airport-zone branch and a city-centre one can sometimes halve the surcharge, provided you’re willing to add ten minutes to the shuttle ride.

Turning up at the counter with the right documents avoids unnecessary delays: a licence held for at least a year for most hire companies, a credit card in the main driver’s name with a high enough limit to block the deposit, and sometimes proof of address for young drivers. An incomplete file at the counter often leads to an unexpected surcharge or outright refusal.

## Driving with zero-deposit LLD: the trade-off between monthly fee and cash flow

Not putting anything down upfront comes at a price: the monthly fee typically rises by 10 to 15% compared to a contract with an equivalent deposit. For a student budget or a young professional who’d rather keep their cash available than tie it up in a deposit, the zero-deposit option often wins out.

![Handing over the keys to a vehicle hired on a zero-deposit LLD](/images/articles/location-courte-longue-duree-2.webp)

Our page everything about zero-deposit LLD for private individuals details the profiles accepted by providers and the maximum monthly fees allowed based on income. A stable file often carries more weight than the deposit amount in the hire company’s decision.

The annual mileage allowance matters just as much as the monthly fee in the final calculation. A 10,000-kilometre annual limit works for urban use, but a 40-kilometre daily commute quickly exceeds it, with each extra kilometre typically costing between €0.08 and €0.15. Also check what routine maintenance actually covers—oil changes and tyres included or not—before comparing two offers based solely on the advertised monthly fee.

## Hire excess: what it covers and why it has nothing to do with your no-claims bonus

The excess set by the hire company applies in case of an at-fault accident or unidentified damage when the car is returned: it ranges from €800 to over €2,000 depending on the car category, and can usually be bought out for a daily fee. This amount has absolutely no connection to your personal no-claims bonus, governed by articles A.121-1 and A.121-2 of the French Insurance Code, which ranges from 0.50 to 3.50 based on your driving history on your own car insurance policy. The hire company never checks this coefficient: it applies its contractual scale, full stop.

Our page buying out hire car excess compares the buy-out options offered by major chains and the credit cards that already include them, often at no extra cost if the trip is paid for with the right card.

Some premium credit cards—Visa Premier or Gold Mastercard, for example—include insurance that reimburses all or part of this excess in case of an accident, provided the entire trip was paid for with that card. A quick call to the card issuer before accepting a buy-out at the counter can often save you the hire company’s option.

## Licence, points and hire: what follows the driver, not the contract

The hire contract changes nothing on the licence side: the points balance follows the driver, not the hired car. A new driver in the probationary period starts with 6 points instead of 12, over three years in the standard system or eighteen months with additional training taken between the sixth and twelfth month. An offence with points deducted during a hire interrupts the annual top-up, which then restarts from zero until the end of the probationary period. This isn’t trivial for frequent short-term hires: a speeding ticket in a hire car has exactly the same effect on your licence as in your own car.

## Additional insurance at the counter: what they actually cover

At the counter, the agent almost always offers fully comprehensive insurance with no excess, windscreen and window protection, and sometimes tyre damage cover. These options cost an average of €10 to €25 per day; added together, they can double the hire price over a single weekend. Many premium credit cards and personal car insurance policies already cover some of these risks for a temporarily hired vehicle. A call before departure—to your insurer or card issuer—lets you know exactly what’s not already covered and avoids paying twice for the same protection.

## Returning the vehicle: checks that avoid unexpected charges

Most disputes arise when the car is returned. Taking time-stamped photos of all four sides of the car, the dashboard showing the mileage, and the fuel level protects you in case of later disputes. A condition report signed by an agent on site is better than dropping the car off at an automated kiosk outside opening hours, where no one can confirm the vehicle’s condition at the exact moment of return. In case of disagreement over a scratch or dent, this evidence lets you challenge an unfair charge directly with the hire company’s customer service before they debit your card.

## Mistakes that inflate the bill, whatever the contract

Some habits prove costly, whether for short or long-term hire.

![Signing a car hire contract with an excess clause](/images/articles/location-courte-longue-duree-3.webp)

-   Ignoring the photo condition report at pick-up: without time-stamped proof, the hire company can charge for any pre-existing scratches on return.
-   Accepting the hire company’s fuel top-up instead of filling up yourself just before returning the car.
-   Signing up for extra insurance at the counter without checking if your credit card or personal car insurance already covers it.
-   Choosing an LLD based solely on the advertised monthly fee, without reading the included mileage or the cost of extra kilometres.
-   Overlooking the exact fuel level stated in the pick-up contract—most hire companies charge for a full tank at an inflated rate if the level on return is below the starting level.

In all five cases, the written contract overrides anything said at the counter. Reading it before signing avoids almost all the nasty surprises listed above.
